Key summary

Quizzy McQuizface is a live, multiplayer pub quiz app for iOS and the web. A host builds a quiz of four or more rounds, and each round can be a topic written by AI, a topic matched instantly from a hand curated bank of 3,130 questions, or a round the host writes themselves. Everyone joins from their own phone with a short code, answers against a countdown and watches a live leaderboard build to the final results. There are no accounts; a session lives for as long as the game does. I took it from competitor research all the way to a live app on the App Store, and nearly every decision along the way came from research or from watching real people play it.

The Idea

The Gap in the Market

Before any design work started, I looked at the quiz apps that already existed. The market split cleanly into two camps, and neither of them did the whole job.

StrengthFrictionless join by PIN and a huge library

GapAI generation is generic, not topic specific

SpeedQuizzing

StrengthBuilt for real pub venues, with team play

GapNo AI generation, and the host needs a laptop

StrengthCode or QR join, teams and a live leaderboard

GapNo AI generation

GapSingle player, with no live social layer

Nobody combined genuinely specific topics with a live, social, team based game. So the round topic input, where a host can type something as specific as “English Premier League, 2012 onwards” and get accurately scoped questions back, became the hero interaction of the whole app.

Research Goals

I wanted to find the gap the existing apps were leaving, understand what hosts and players each need from a quiz night, and find the moments in a game that matter most to the people playing it.

User Personas

Three personas anchored every decision that came after.

🎤

Persona #1 · Host

The Quiz Master

The host. Wants a genuinely specific, good quiz running in minutes, needs confidence that a specific request gives specific questions, and wants visible control over difficulty and pacing.

Who

Mid 20s to 40s, and organises the social calendar for their friend group or work team. Has run a pub quiz off a laptop or a WhatsApp group before.

Goals
  • Get a genuinely good, specific quiz running in under 3 minutes
  • Look good in front of the group, so questions need to land, not embarrass them with a dud round
Frustrations
  • Generic quiz apps give bland, overly easy or repeated questions
  • Setting up teams and manually tracking scores is a hassle
Needs from the app
  • Fast round creation
  • Confidence that a specific input produces specific output
  • Visible control over difficulty and pacing
  • An easy way to referee a tie
📱

Persona #2 · Player

The Player

Wants to join in seconds with no account, understand what to do straight away, and get clear feedback that their answer has registered.

Who

Any age, and joining because a friend sent them a code. Zero patience for setup friction.

Goals
  • Join in under 10 seconds
  • Understand what to do immediately
  • Have fun, and not feel stupid if they don’t know an answer
Frustrations
  • Apps that need an account just to join one session
  • Confusing team joining flows
  • Not knowing if their answer registered
Needs from the app
  • Name entry only, with no forced sign up
  • A code field that forgives case and spaces
  • Clear team selection
  • Immediate answer feedback
🏆

Persona #3 · Secondary

The Regular

Plays every week with the same group. Cares about difficulty actually feeling different from tier to tier, and about a tie breaker that feels fair rather than random.

Who

Plays weekly with the same group, is competitive, and cares about the leaderboard and the tie breaker.

Goals
  • Consistent difficulty from round to round
  • A fair and satisfying tie breaker
  • Bragging rights
Needs from the app
  • Difficulty tiers that actually feel different in practice, not just in name
  • A tie breaker that feels legitimate rather than arbitrary, like a closest number or sudden death question

The review moment

A host checking the AI questions before players see them is the biggest trust risk in the whole flow, and it spoils the quiz if the host wants to play too. So I added a “host also plays” toggle, which skips the human review and runs an automated quality check instead.

The winner moment

The results screen is the emotional peak of the night. That is why the ad sits after the celebration rather than before it, and why “Play again” resets the same lobby instead of sending everyone back to the start.

Core Flows

Hosting a quiz

  1. Enter a name, then choose to create a quiz.
  2. Pick solo or team mode, the number of rounds, questions per round and difficulty.
  3. Build each round from a topic, or write one by hand, with a personal library of saved rounds to reuse later.
  4. Check each round has generated, with a clear retry or edit option for anything that failed.
  5. Land in a lobby with a join code, watch players and teams join live, then start.
  6. Play through live, then see the results and leaderboard, with one tap to play again in the same lobby.

Joining a quiz

  1. Enter a name, then a join code.
  2. Pick a team if the host has chosen team mode.
  3. Wait in the lobby, watching everyone else arrive.
  4. Play through live, then see the same results screen as the host.

Game Mechanics as Design Decisions

Some of the game rules involved real trade offs, so I treated them as design decisions rather than technical details.

Scoring

Every question has a 15 second timer, and points fall from 500 to 100 the longer you take. A correct answer always scores at least 100 and a wrong answer always scores zero, so a careful answer beats a fast guess and nobody learns to just tap the first option.

A fair reveal

Nobody finds out whether they were right until everyone has answered or the timer runs out. Otherwise a fast player’s phone could give the answer away to slower teammates sitting next to them.

Moving on

A question ends as soon as everyone has answered, followed by a short animated transition. I first cut straight to the next question, but players told me it felt jarring.

Teams

The host picks how many teams and players are split as evenly as possible. If the host gets distracted, a 30 second countdown sorts the teams automatically, and every team gets a playful name instead of Team 1, 2 and 3.

Tie breakers

Because wrong answers score zero, exact ties happen more often than you would think, so ties are settled by a sudden death question where the closest number wins.

Question Sourcing

The question bank started as a problem, not a plan. Generating every round live with AI kept hitting rate limits during real games. Rather than just paying for a bigger quota, I changed the design: common topics like “Movies”, “80s music” or “Premier League” now come instantly from a hand curated bank of 3,130 questions across 13 categories, with no wait and no cost, and only genuinely specific topics go to ChatGPT.

I built the categories out in a lot of detail, with film and TV franchises, music split by decade and by genre, and sport split by discipline, so that a host’s topic hits the fast, free path as often as possible.

Writing Your Own Rounds

Hosts can also write rounds by hand, and a few decisions went into making that feel easy.

Its own doorway

Writing a full round takes several minutes, so I moved it from a small third option on the round card to its own entry on the Settings screen.

One question at a time

Each question is its own page with a title, the question, four options and the correct answer, which gives a clear sense of progress on an effortful task.

Autosave

Nothing is lost if a host backs out halfway through writing.

Tonight’s copy

Editing a round in tonight’s quiz only changes that copy, so a quick tweak never overwrites the saved version for next time.

Not yet: sharing

I considered letting hosts share rounds, but deliberately left it for later, as it needs its own moderation and ownership rules.

Trust and Safety

Round topics and player and team names are free text that everyone in the game can see. That makes them user generated content, and Apple’s App Store review requires it to be handled properly, so I designed for it on two sides.

Before anyone sees it

Topics, team names and player names are all checked before they are submitted. Round topics also feed straight into the AI, so checking them protects against prompt injection as well as bad language.

Once it is visible

Anyone can report a name or topic from the lobby, and a host can remove a disruptive player, who is disconnected and sent back to the start with a clear message. Every one of these actions is rate limited, so the tools for handling abuse cannot be abused themselves.

When the AI Gets It Wrong

AI generation can fail for reasons a generic error message does not explain; the daily quota runs out, a topic has no real facts to quiz on, or the content gets blocked. So instead of one “something went wrong” message, each failure explains itself and offers the right next step.

A blocked topic

The host is asked to rephrase the topic and try again.

Nothing to ask about

The app explains why, and hides the Retry button entirely. Retrying would fail in exactly the same way, so offering it would be a false promise.

Anything else

A plain, simple retry.

I also tell people plainly when AI is involved. A one time notice on the round builder explains that topics needing AI are sent to a provider that may use them for training, and it never appears again once dismissed.

Look and Feel

The Design System

The final system uses a single navy accent with white and pale blue neutrals, and one deliberate exception: a warm gold that only appears when the timer is running low. Everything sits on an 8pt grid, with two named exceptions; 44pt tap targets to match Apple’s guidelines, and a toggle switch sized to match the platform standard. Headings use Fredoka, a rounded and friendly typeface, against Roboto for body text, on a six step type scale with nothing smaller than 12px. The same handful of cards, pills and corner radii carry across every screen.

Getting there took a lot of testing for a second accent colour, until I realised two bold filled buttons compete for attention whatever colour the second one is. So every screen has one filled button, with everything else outlined in the same navy.

Accessibility, Built In

Contrast

Every colour pairing actually used in the app was checked against WCAG AA, not just a few spot checks.

One focus indicator

I found and fixed a bug where text inputs showed two focus indicators at once, a colour change and an outline, and cut it down to one clear indicator per control.

Quiet emoji

Decorative icons and emoji are hidden from screen readers, so they read the text beside them instead of announcing “trophy” or “warning sign”.

Reduced motion

Transitions and status animations respect the reduced motion setting.

Testing

Testing on a Real Quiz Night

I ran informal usability testing with a group of target users in a live social setting, watching real multiplayer games, how people joined, and how engaged they stayed. What I saw fed straight into refining the onboarding and the flow of a session.

The Web Redesign

The problem

The web version was simply the iPhone layout stretched or boxed onto a desktop screen. Every screen, from a simple choice screen to a live lobby, sat at roughly phone width however wide the browser was. Buttons either stayed phone sized in a sea of empty space or stretched into huge pills that looked accidental rather than designed.

The goals

  • Change the web version only, leaving the iOS app exactly as it was
  • Real multi column layouts where the content benefits, not just a wider single column
  • Comfortable line lengths, tap target sizes, zoom support and a reading order that matches the layout
  • Buttons stay a normal size at any width, while cards and fields stretch to use the space
  • A full width colour banner across the top of the browser

Six rounds of iteration

  1. A centred columnEvery screen went into one centred column. It was the right instinct, but it still looked like a phone in a box on a wide monitor.
  2. Real multi column layoutsScreens that benefit, like the round builder, library, lobby and results, got true multi column layouts, while simple forms stayed single column but wider.
  3. A full width bannerThe top banner stretched across the whole browser, and the boxed blue page frame became plain white margins.
  4. What stretches and what does notCards and inputs grow with the page, while buttons got their own width limit so they never grow just because their container does.
  5. Fixing what testing foundLive testing showed a few regressions, including the standings card popping in beside the question and shifting it on the reveal, so it went back to sitting underneath.
  6. One last inconsistencyA footer with two buttons side by side had inherited the single button width limit, making those buttons smaller than every other button in the app, so it got its own wider option.

Key decisions

DecisionWhy
One shared breakpointEvery layout decision checks the same number, so screens never switch layout inconsistently.
Buttons set their own widthCards and inputs can use the extra space on a wide screen without dragging the button size along with them.
Reading order fixed in the codeScreen reader and keyboard order always matches the visual order at every width, rather than relying on a CSS trick.
Standings stay under the questionA card popping in beside the question was distracting, and stacking kept the feel players already knew.

“We Couldn’t Ready Up”

Playtesting the new lobby turned up a real gameplay bug, not just a visual one. If a player’s connection dropped between rounds, the rest of the group could not ready up for the next round, and the game made everyone wait out a 45 second safety timer. To the players, it looked like the game had frozen.

The cause was a fix for an earlier bug, where a brief wifi blip let the game skip a player before they had a fair chance to press ready. I solved it by reusing a pattern the app already used mid question: a disconnected player only stops counting once they have been gone for a few seconds. A quick blip still gets a fair chance, but a real disconnect no longer holds the whole group hostage.

Director’s commentary

The bug report came straight from a live game, word for word: “we couldn’t ready up for the next round”.

Every change was checked in the browser at desktop and mobile widths, including a read out of the accessibility tree to confirm the reading order. The fix was proven with a new automated test that recreates the exact scenario, and all 681 automated tests stayed green throughout.

Key Takeaways

Challenge

The hardest part was holding myself to evidence. It is tempting to polish the things you think might be wrong, but nearly every fix in this project traces back to a specific moment where a real player got confused or stuck, or to a specific piece of research. Designing and building it myself also meant I felt every trade off between what was ideal and what was possible.

Lessons Learned

Ship it, then watch

Putting something reasonable in front of real players taught me more than any amount of guessing about what might work.

Check the question

After testing more than a dozen colours, the real answer was realising I was asking the wrong question.

Constraints can be features

A rate limit problem became the question bank, and the fastest, cheapest path became the best experience for most hosts.
