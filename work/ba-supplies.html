Key summary

BA Supplies and Services is a family-run firm that has designed, supplied, installed and serviced commercial kitchens across London and the South East since 1984. Their old website was outdated, half-built and running on Joomla, with empty product pages and no analytics. Using Claude AI throughout the process, I redesigned it as a focused four-page site built around trust and enquiries; clean, responsive, accessible, and phone-first. I then built it in code and launched it on their own domain with GA4 tracking so their success could finally be measured.

UX Research

Overview

This project started with a conversation with the owner. BA has a strong reputation built over 40 years, but almost all of their work came through word of mouth; recommendations, repeat clients, and people who already knew them. The owner’s goal was simple: he wanted more of an online presence, a website that actually represented the quality of the business and could win work from people who found them online for the first time.

Research Goals

I wanted to understand what the business really does, who its customers are, and why the current site wasn’t pulling its weight; so that the redesign could be built around real goals rather than just “make it look nicer”.

User Interviews

Because this is a small business, my primary research was a series of interviews with the owner — the person who knows the customers, the enquiries and the sales process better than anyone. He explained how clients typically find and choose BA, what questions they ask, and what he felt the old site was missing. Two themes came through immediately: nearly all business was referral-based, and the site did nothing to convert the people who did land on it. From there I audited the existing site myself and looked at how competitors present themselves.

The old website

This is the old Joomla site as I found it at the start of the project.

Major issues

  • The “products for sale” pages were empty. The product sections had nothing in them — implying an online shop the business doesn’t actually run, and leaving visitors at a dead end.

  • The site felt unfinished and old-fashioned, with dated copy (it still referenced “the last 28 years”).

  • The Joomla build didn’t hold up on phones — where a lot of urgent, on-site users actually are.

  • There was no analytics at all, so the business had no idea how many people visited, what they did, or whether the site ever produced a single enquiry.

Minor issues

  • Phone number buried

    The single most valuable action for an urgent-service business wasn’t prominent.

  • Cluttered navigation

    A “Special Offers” page and product sub-menus added noise without value.

  • Proof hidden

    A Civic Trust Award and big-name clients were buried instead of building trust.

  • Inconsistent styling

    Pages didn’t share a coherent look, adding to the unfinished feel.

Research Findings

The phone

For an urgent-service business, a visible, one-tap phone number matters more than anything else on the page.

It’s not a shop

The site should sell trust and generate enquiries — not pretend to be an e-commerce catalogue.

A modern site is credible design

When you sell kitchen design, your own site is a proof point. A dated site quietly loses the sale.

Proof wins B2B

Named clients and the award are strong differentiators — they need to be seen, not buried.

UX Design

Prioritisation and Project Goals

The owner’s goals were about the business — more presence, more enquiries, looking professional. My job was to line those up against what actual users need, and design where the two overlap. These goals were:

  • Build a credible online presence
  • Generate more enquiries and phone calls
  • Look as professional as possible
  • Instantly reach someone by phone to enquire
  • Understand what the company does with one look of the webpage

Information Architecture

The biggest structural decision was removing things. The old site’s empty “Products” and “Special Offers” pages implied an e-commerce model BA doesn’t run. I cut them and reduced the site to four focused pages, with the service story deep-linked so the home page can point straight to the right section.

User Flows

I immediately identified the most important user flow; the user finding the phone number and calling BA Supplies.

Low Fidelity Wireframes

In the absence of having a low fidelity software like Balsamiq mock ups, I chose to use pen and paper to quickly draw up a visual hierarchy of the components on the page.

UI Design

UI Library Components with Claude

Using the research I had already instructed Claude to do, I then instructed Claude to build a small, consistent design system so every page would share the same language and be quick to build. I based the visual direction on Material 3; clean surfaces, soft radii and a clear type scale. I chose a palette that does real work: deep blue for trust, amber for action. Every primary step (call, request a quote) is amber so the eye is always drawn to it. I researched current standards and set the whole system on an 8-point spacing grid.

Usability Testing

Prototype

Because the prototype was the real site, I could test it on real devices with the people who matter most. I ran informal task-based sessions with the owner and a handful of his clients — the kind of facilities managers and chefs the personas represent. I gave them simple tasks: find the phone number and call, request a quote, and check whether BA serves their sector. Overall the feedback was positive, and a few clear points came out of it.

✅What worked

  • The sticky, one-tap phone was found instantly on mobile

  • The ‘design, supply, install, service’ story answered the “do you do the whole job?” question up front

  • Everyone described it as looking “far more professional”

⚠️Needs improving

  • Some users paused over “main” vs “emergency” number

  • The “we service all makes” message was too easy to miss

  • Clients wanted to see real project photos, not just read about them

💡Ideas

  • Surface “all makes servicing” on the home page

  • Set up GA4 to track data on the website

Setting up GA4

Challenge

One of the biggest problems with the old site was invisible: there was no data at all. The business genuinely didn’t know if the website ever produced a single enquiry. So a key deliverable was setting up Google Analytics 4 from launch — installing the tracking across all four pages and creating a property so, for the first time, BA has a baseline to measure against.

Lessons Learned

Because there was no legacy analytics, the redesign’s impact can only be measured forward from launch, so I set GA4 up on day one and planned a 30/60/90-day review of enquiries, click-to-call taps and quote submissions.

What we now track: page views and traffic sources (is the new “presence” actually bringing people in?), click-to-call taps — especially on mobile — and quote-form submissions, the two actions that map directly to the owner’s goal of more enquiries.

Key Takeaways

Challenge

The hardest part wasn’t visual — it was resisting the urge to add. It would have been easy to build a big, feature-heavy site, but the right move for this business was the opposite: strip it back. Deleting the empty product pages and reducing the site to four focused pages made it far more effective, not less. Knowing what to leave out was the real design decision.

Lessons Learned

Build the system first

Designing the components and tokens up front made every page consistent and turned later changes into one-line edits across the whole site.

Removing is designing

The highest-value decision was taking things away. A smaller, sharper site did the business more good than a bigger one.

Measure from day one

Without analytics there’s no baseline and no way to prove impact. Set up tracking before launch, not after.

Real photos beat stock

The clearest feedback from clients was wanting to see genuine work — real project photography is the next step and the biggest content win.
