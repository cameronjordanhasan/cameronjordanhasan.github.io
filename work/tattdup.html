TattdUp

An AI tattoo generator that lets you take a photo of your body and move the design around it, so you can see how it will look before you ever contact a tattoo artist.

Key summary

TattdUp is an AI tattoo design app built around a single hard problem: helping someone trust a permanent decision made by an unpredictable machine. You describe an idea or upload a reference photo, refine the design the AI creates, then try it on a photo of your own skin, dragging, pinching and rotating it into place. I did everything myself, from the research and product strategy to the flows, the design system, the accessibility work, the monetisation model, the copy and the build. Almost every decision in the app follows from that one idea of trust.

The Idea

The Challenge

A tattoo is permanent, so the gap between “I have an idea” and “I trust this enough to put it on my body” is the whole product problem. Two things had to be true at once.

It has to be tattooable

The artwork needs closed linework, high contrast and a design that stays readable once ink spreads and softens with age. A pretty AI image is not enough.

You have to see it on you

People need to see the design on their own skin before they commit, without having to book an artist first.

The Gap in the Market

Before I drew a single screen, I looked at the existing AI tattoo tools. Most of them stop at generating a picture and treat a tattoo like any other AI image. That misses what makes tattoos different: the picture is not the end product, a permanent mark on a body is. Generators that make attractive images but do nothing to help you judge whether you would actually wear it left a clear gap, and that gap is what TattdUp aims at.

What competitors do badly

😶 They stop at the image

The hardest part of getting a tattoo is not the picture, it is deciding whether it belongs on your body. Very few apps carry you through to seeing it on your own skin.

🔁 They regenerate rather than refine

Most offer a whole new design rather than improving the one you already like. Changing one part while keeping the rest is rare.

😠 Their subscriptions make people angry

Reviews are full of people placed into a subscription after a trial they felt they could not avoid, then charged after cancelling.

Choosing the AI Model

Picking the image model was a real part of the work, and it came down to cost and quality rather than taste. I compared several models on their price per image and on whether they could edit an existing image, not just create a new one.

My first plan used two engines, a cheaper one for free users and a stronger one for paying users, which conveniently made editing a paid feature by necessity. Two findings overturned it. The cheap engine was being retired, and testing showed a single mid tier model was good enough and cheap enough for everyone. So I moved to one engine for all users, which then forced an honest rethink of why editing should be paid at all.

User Personas

I anchored the design to four types of user, and each one drove a real decision.

😬

Persona #1 · Ellie, 24, Manchester

The First Timer

Nervous and unsure, has never been tattooed and is testing whether they even dare. The try on exists largely for them, and so does guest entry, so they can explore without making an account.

Who

Ellie has wanted a tattoo for about two years and has never booked one. She has a folder of screenshots and a vague sense she wants something botanical and fine line on her forearm. What stops her is not the money, it is the permanence.

What they need

Confidence. The moment that matters to her is seeing the design on a photo of her own arm, because that is the first time the idea becomes real.

What they shaped

The try on with a real photo, and a one off purchase so she is never pushed into a subscription.

🔁

Persona #2 · Sam, 31, Bristol

The Iterator

Has a clear idea and wants to refine it again and again until it is exactly right. Region editing and the credit pack are shaped around them, and they are the most likely to pay.

Who

Sam already has three tattoos and knows exactly what he wants for the fourth. He will decide the linework on one wing is too heavy and want to change only that, keeping everything else identical.

What they need

Refinement, not regeneration. A brand new design throws away the parts he liked.

What they shaped

Region editing, where you mark one part of the design and change only that.

🖋️

Persona #3 · Priya, 38, London

The Artist

Cares about whether the output is genuinely usable as a tattoo, which is why tattooability, not just image quality, became the central product risk.

Who

Priya runs a small studio. She uses AI to sketch concepts quickly for clients during consultations, then draws the real thing herself, generating a lot in short bursts.

What they need

Output she can genuinely work from as a tattoo, not just an attractive image.

What they shaped

Tattooability as the central product risk, and a capped Pro plan, because an unlimited one could cost more to run than she pays.

👀

Persona #4 · Josh, 19, Leeds

The Browser

Curious but not committed, uses the free tier and may never pay. The watermark and free allowance are designed for them, because a shared, watermarked design is free marketing.

Who

Josh is not getting a tattoo. He wants to see what a dragon on his arm would look like for a laugh, sends it to a group chat and never opens the app again.

What they need

A quick, fun result with no sign up wall in the way.

What they shaped

The free generation limit, and the visible watermark, so every screenshot he shares advertises the app.

Designing It

The Core Flow

Seeing the design on your own skin shaped the whole journey into three stages, with a stepper on screen the whole time so you always know where you are and what is left.

  1. Idea

    Describe your tattoo in words, or upload a reference photo.

  2. Design

    The AI creates the artwork, and you refine it until it is right.

  3. Try On

    Place the design on a photo of your own skin, or a sample skin tone, then drag, pinch and rotate it into position.

That last step is the moment an abstract picture turns into a real question: would I actually get this?

User Flow

Behind the three stages sits one important decision point. Before a single generation is spent, the app checks the prompt is worth drawing and that there is something left to spend.

You tap Generate design

  • ✋ Too thin to draw

    A keyboard mash, a single letter or a word with no subject. A friendly nudge offers three example prompts. No credit used.

  • 🔒 Nothing left to spend

    The paywall opens instead of generating.

  • ✨ Good to go

    The design is generated and one generation is used.

  1. Step 1

    Idea

    • Describe the idea in your own words, or work from a reference photo
    • Surprise me for a random prompt, Enhance my idea for a fuller brief
    • Pick style, placement, size, detail and shading, all with sensible defaults

  2. Step 2

    Design

    • A generating screen shows your own words while it works
    • The design appears on cream flash paper
    • Rework the whole design by typing, or edit one region with Draw or Box
    • Edits stack and undo one at a time
    • Save to your Sketchbook

  3. Step 3

    Try on

    • Take or choose a photo, or use one of six sample skin tones
    • Drag, pinch and twist the design into place
    • Set the opacity to judge how heavy the ink sits
    • Download, watermarked on the free plan and clean on a paid one

Paid only when it works

A generation is only used when an image is actually produced. A blocked prompt, a failed one or an abandoned journey costs nothing.

Free if it costs nothing to run

The try on, positioning, the watermarked download and the Sketchbook all run on the phone, so they are free for everyone.

Three ways to the paywall

Running out of generations, tapping a locked feature, or asking to remove a watermark. Each one has its own heading.

Designing for Trust, Control and Feedback

If there is one theme that runs through the whole app, it is this. The product is built on a model whose output cannot be fully predicted, applied to a decision that is permanent, so almost every choice is really about trust and control.

Show the cost first

You always see what an action will cost before you commit to it.

Show the working

The app makes it clear when it is working, rather than leaving you staring at a frozen screen.

Correct, don’t just regenerate

You can fix and undo parts of a design, not only start again.

Honest copy

The words always say plainly what is happening and what it costs.

Waiting is a real state

Because the app waits on an AI model, I designed the waiting as carefully as everything else.

A launch screen

So the app never opens on a blank rectangle.

A generating screen

It shows you your own words while you wait, so you know the right thing is being made.

A working overlay

During an edit it covers the artwork and locks the controls, so a slow, paid action can never be triggered twice by an impatient second tap.

The rule underneath all three: anything that costs you money must visibly show that it is running.

Process

I worked through clickable prototypes before any real build, so I could feel and test the flows cheaply before committing to code. I treated the prototype as a working specification, covering every screen, every state and every piece of copy, rather than a throwaway mock up.

I also wrote ten specification documents alongside the design, covering the brief, research, personas, flows, screens and states, component library, design decisions, technical architecture, monetisation strategy, and the open risks and roadmap.

Copy was treated as design too. Several of the sharpest fixes in the project were changes to wording rather than layout, and the rule I followed was that the interface should never claim something the app no longer does.

Monetisation

There are three plans on the paywall, cheapest to most expensive from left to right. The credit pack, not the subscription, is the hero, with a best value badge and stronger styling even though it sits in the middle.

Avoiding the subscription trap

I led with a one time purchase rather than pushing people towards a recurring charge, and made sure cancelling or switching plans never feels punitive. The copy says plainly that any generations you have bought carry over, even if your plan changes or lapses.

Paid by choice, not necessity

Editing was first paid because the cheap engine could not edit. Once one engine served everyone, that reason disappeared, so rather than pretend otherwise I gave free users a small number of real edits. People buy what they have experienced, not what they have merely been told about.

The same allowance

An edit costs the same to run as a new design, so it spends from the same allowance, and the app says so plainly rather than hiding it.

A deliberate non change

When you run out of generations after finishing a design, Try it on stays the main button and Get more generations stays secondary, even though the second one makes money. Your goal at that moment is finishing what you came to do.

Interaction Design Challenges

Dragging a design into place, drawing a region to edit and sliding the opacity control all happen on a screen that scrolls. On a real phone the scroll kept winning, so a finger meant to move the tattoo moved the whole page instead. The textbook fix of telling the gesture to take priority looked correct but did not hold up on the device. What actually worked was more direct: the screen switches scrolling off the moment one of these gestures begins.

That change then broke something else. A resize handle inside the draggable box stopped responding, because the box was now winning every touch, including the ones meant for its own handle. The fix was not a cleverer rule. I moved the handle to sit beside the box instead of inside it, so the two never compete for the same touch.

Director’s commentary

When two things are fighting over the same gesture, moving one out of the other’s way works better than adding another rule to referee the fight.

Look and Feel

The Design System

The palette went through five complete directions before it settled on the warm charcoal, gilt amber and jade green that shipped. The visual language runs on one rule: two colours, two jobs, never blurred.

Gilt amber means tap this

It is the only action colour in the app, used on every button and tappable control. Its text is always dark, never white, so it reads as metal or ink rather than a generic app blue.

Jade green means paid

It is the only signal for a paid or premium feature, and it is used nowhere else, so you can tell at a glance what costs money.

Charcoal and flash paper

The ground is a warm charcoal, and the artwork sits on a cream tone, a nod to the flash sheets in a tattoo studio rather than a plain white canvas.

Type follows a six step scale of 12, 14, 16, 20, 24 and 40 pixels, with nothing smaller than 12. Every text input is at least 16 pixels, which stops iOS Safari zooming the page when you tap into one. A display face, Pirata One, is saved for the wordmark and a handful of screen titles, and everything else, including every button, uses Inter. It feels like a tattoo brand without giving up legibility.

Spacing follows a strict 8 point grid with one deliberate exception, 44 pixels, the minimum accessible tap target. When an early pass drifted, with a guest account’s save and delete buttons sitting at different spacing, I treated it as a real bug rather than polish and regrouped them on the same 8 point rhythm.

Accessibility

Editing part of a design starts with a freehand loop you trace around the area with a finger, but that gesture is not usable by everyone, so it was never allowed to be the only way in. A Draw or Box control offers a second option: a rectangle you place with a tap and resize with simple drags. I treated this as a launch requirement, and editing did not ship until both worked.

A full WCAG 2.2 contrast check caught two real failures. A muted grey text colour looked fine but measured below the 4.5 to 1 needed for body text, so I lightened it until it passed. The borders on outlined buttons, inputs and cards were so close to the background that they failed the 3 to 1 minimum for non text contrast, so I swapped them for a properly visible colour.

Beyond the audit, every control has a visible focus ring, tap targets are at least 44 pixels across the app, purely decorative symbols are hidden from screen readers, and icon only buttons have clear labels.

Testing

Refinements From Real Use

Testing on real devices surfaced a run of small, specific confusions. I fixed each one as a genuine UX bug rather than leaving it as polish for later.

Generations left

Before
A subscriber saw two different “generations left” numbers in different places, which looked contradictory.
After
One number, shown the same way on every screen.

Guest sign out

Before
A guest’s sign out button added a confusing extra step with no real benefit.
After
Removed for guests, and deleting a guest account now goes straight to a real sign in.

Region edit copy

Before
The copy said “2 free edits remaining”, a limit that no longer existed.
After
A simple note that each region edit uses a generation, whatever your plan.

Change photo

Before
Changing your photo in Try On went through an extra screen before the picker even opened.
After
It opens the photo or camera picker straight away, and is hidden when you are using a sample skin tone.

The back button

Before
Back in Try On skipped a whole step.
After
It now steps back exactly one screen, matching what the stepper shows.

Watermark removal

Before
Paying to remove a watermark changed nothing else you could see, so there was no sign it had worked.
After
A small label confirms the watermark has been removed wherever it would otherwise show.

The common thread: a fix only counted once it matched what the app had promised elsewhere. One number, one step per tap, and one consistent signal for a paid action.

Key Takeaways

Challenge

The hardest part was designing trust around something I could not fully control. The AI’s output is unpredictable and the decision it feeds is permanent, so every screen had to earn a little more confidence, by showing costs up front, making the waiting visible and never saying anything the app no longer does. Doing all of it solo, from research to build, meant every trade off between what was ideal and what was possible landed on my desk.

Lessons Learned

Trust is the product

When the output is unpredictable, the design around it, like costs, feedback and undo, matters as much as the output itself.

Name your risks

Writing down a risk I had not fully solved, with a backup ready, was more useful than pretending it was not there.

Structure beats rules

Moving things out of each other’s way fixed gesture conflicts that extra rules could not.

Copy is design

Some of the most important fixes were a few words that stopped the app promising something it no longer did.

Where It Stands

TattdUp is live on the App Store, taken from a blank page to a working product single handed. It is still too early for usage numbers, so this case study focuses on the reasoning behind the design, and I will add a results section once real users arrive.
